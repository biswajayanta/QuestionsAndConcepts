# Class 10 English Grammar — Concept Document (Parent's Copy)
### Mid-Term portion: Determiners, Tenses, Modals, Subject–Verb Concord, Reported Speech

*This covers the rule, the way CBSE actually tests it (gap-fill/editing/dialogue-completion format), and the traps students commonly fall into for each unit.*

---

## Unit 1 — Determiners

**What they are:** Words that come before a noun to specify quantity, definiteness, or possession — articles (a, an, the), demonstratives (this, that, these, those), quantifiers (some, any, much, many, few, little, a few, a little, all, both, either, neither, each, every), and possessives (my, your, his, her, its, our, their).

**Key rules CBSE tests:**
1. **a/an** — "a" before consonant *sounds* (a university — "yoo" sound; a European), "an" before vowel *sounds* (an hour — silent h; an MBA — "em" sound). It's about sound, not the letter.
2. **much/many, little/few** — "much" and "little" go with uncountable nouns (much water, little time); "many" and "few" go with countable plural nouns (many books, few chances).
3. **a few / few** and **a little / little** — "a few/a little" = some (positive sense); "few/little" (without "a") = almost none (negative sense). E.g., "I have a few friends" (some, fine) vs. "I have few friends" (almost none, lonely).
4. **each vs every** — "each" is used for two or more, emphasizes individuality; "every" is used for three or more, emphasizes the whole group collectively. Both take a singular verb.
5. **the** — used for something already specified/known, superlatives, unique things (the sun), and specific mentions; omitted for general/plural/uncountable references (I like music, not "I like the music" unless referring to specific music already mentioned).

**Where students go wrong:** Confusing "a/an" with spelling instead of sound; using "much/many" with the wrong noun type; missing that "little" (no article) has a negative meaning while "a little" is positive; forgetting articles are often tested inside a full sentence/dialogue, not standalone.

## Unit 2 — Tenses

**What they are:** Verb forms showing time of action — CBSE Class 10 typically tests Present (simple/continuous/perfect/perfect continuous), Past (simple/continuous/perfect/perfect continuous), and Future forms, usually via gap-fill in a passage/dialogue, not isolated rule questions.

**Key rules CBSE tests:**
1. **Simple Present** — habitual actions, general truths, timetables (She *walks* to school. The sun *rises* in the east.)
2. **Present Continuous** — action happening now, or a planned near-future action (She *is walking* right now. We *are leaving* tomorrow.)
3. **Present Perfect** — action completed in the recent past with present relevance, or an action that started in the past and continues (I *have finished* my homework. She *has lived* here for 5 years.) — uses "has/have + past participle."
4. **Past Simple vs Past Continuous** — simple past = a completed action at a definite past time (He *went* to the market yesterday); past continuous = an ongoing action interrupted by another, or two simultaneous past actions (He *was going* to the market when it started raining.)
5. **Past Perfect** — an action completed *before* another past action (She *had left* before he arrived) — the "earlier of two past events" signal.
6. **Sequence of tenses** — the biggest trap area: when a sentence has two clauses, the tense in one often constrains the tense in the other (e.g., "If" clauses/conditionals, time clauses with "before/after/when/since").

**Where students go wrong:** Mixing up present perfect ("has done") with simple past ("did") — present perfect needs present relevance or an unspecified time, simple past needs a specific past time marker (yesterday, last year, in 2020); not recognizing "since" (point in time) vs "for" (duration) with perfect tenses; getting past perfect vs simple past backwards when two past actions are mentioned.

## Unit 3 — Modals

**What they are:** Auxiliary verbs that express necessity, possibility, permission, ability, obligation, or advice — can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would, ought to, need, dare.

**Key rules CBSE tests:**
1. **can/could** — ability (can = present ability, could = past ability or polite request); permission (can = informal, could = more polite/formal).
2. **may/might** — permission (may, more formal than can) and possibility (may = more likely, might = less likely/more hypothetical).
3. **must/have to** — must = strong obligation from the speaker's own judgment or a strict rule (You *must* wear a seatbelt); have to = obligation from external circumstances/authority (I *have to* submit the form by Friday — the deadline, not my opinion, makes it necessary). "Must not" = prohibition; "don't have to" = no obligation (very different meanings — a common trap!).
4. **should/ought to** — advice or moral obligation, roughly interchangeable, though "should" is more common in speech.
5. **need not** — absence of necessity (You *need not* come if you're busy).
6. **would** — polite requests, habitual past actions ("would" for repeated past habit, similar to "used to"), and conditional sentences.

**Where students go wrong:** Confusing "must not" (prohibition — forbidden) with "need not / don't have to" (no obligation — permitted but optional) — this is the single most common modal trap in CBSE papers; using "could" and "was able to" interchangeably (they're not always the same — "could" for general past ability, "was able to" often preferred for a specific one-time successful action, though CBSE Class 10 level usually accepts either for general use).

## Unit 4 — Subject–Verb Concord (Agreement)

**What it is:** The verb must agree in number (singular/plural) and person with its subject.

**Key rules CBSE tests:**
1. **Basic agreement** — singular subject → singular verb (He *runs*); plural subject → plural verb (They *run*).
2. **Collective nouns** — usually take a singular verb when treated as a single unit (The team *is* playing well), but can take a plural verb when members are considered individually/acting separately (The team *are* arguing among themselves) — CBSE usually tests the singular-unit sense.
3. **Indefinite pronouns** — each, everyone, everybody, everything, someone, anybody, nobody, either, neither → always singular verb, even if they "feel" plural (Everyone *is* ready. Neither of the boys *is* absent.)
4. **Two subjects joined by "and"** → usually plural verb (Ram and Shyam *are* friends) — UNLESS they refer to a single idea/entity (Bread and butter *is* my breakfast — treated as one item).
5. **Subjects joined by "or/nor", "either...or", "neither...nor"** → the verb agrees with the subject **nearer to it** (Neither the teacher nor the students *were* present — verb agrees with "students," the nearer subject).
6. **Phrases between subject and verb** (e.g., "along with," "as well as," "together with," "in addition to") do NOT change the number of the verb — the verb still agrees with the main subject, ignoring the inserted phrase (The captain, along with his teammates, *was* honoured — singular, because "the captain" is the actual subject).
7. **"A number of" vs "the number of"** — "a number of" (meaning "many") takes a plural verb (A number of students *were* absent); "the number of" (referring to a specific count) takes a singular verb (The number of students *was* 40).

**Where students go wrong:** Being distracted by a plural-looking word sitting right before the verb (a phrase inserted between subject and verb) and matching the verb to that nearby word instead of the actual subject; getting "either...or / neither...nor" agreement backwards (should match the nearer subject, not the first one); treating indefinite pronouns as plural just because they refer to a group of people.

## Unit 5 — Reported Speech (Statements, Questions, Commands, Requests)

**What it is:** Converting direct speech (someone's exact words in quotes) into indirect/reported speech (retelling what was said, without quotation marks) — requires changes to tense, pronouns, time/place references, and sentence structure depending on whether it's a statement, question, command, or request.

**Key rules CBSE tests:**

1. **Reporting verb changes:** "says/say" → stays as "says/say" (no backshift needed) if reporting verb is present tense; "said" → the reported clause's tense usually shifts **one step back** (backshift): Present Simple → Past Simple; Present Continuous → Past Continuous; Present Perfect → Past Perfect; Past Simple → Past Perfect; "will" → "would"; "can" → "could"; "may" → "might"; "must" → "had to" (for obligation).

2. **Pronoun changes:** First person pronouns (I, we) change according to the subject of the reporting verb; second person pronouns (you) change according to the object of the reporting verb; third person pronouns usually stay the same.

3. **Time and place word changes** (only if the reporting is happening at a different time/place than the original speech):
   - now → then/at that time
   - today → that day
   - tomorrow → the next day/the following day
   - yesterday → the previous day/the day before
   - here → there
   - this → that; these → those
   - ago → before

4. **Statements:** Reporting verb "say/said" → often changes to "said that" (or "told + object + that" if there's a listener mentioned). Structure: Subject + said (to X) that + clause (with backshifted tense).

5. **Questions:**
   - **Yes/No questions:** use "asked/enquired **if/whether**" + the clause in **statement word order** (not question word order) — e.g., "Are you coming?" → He asked if/whether I *was* coming. (Common trap: keeping the question word order — "He asked if was I coming" is WRONG.)
   - **Wh-questions:** use "asked" + the wh-word directly (no "if/whether") + clause in statement word order — e.g., "Where do you live?" → He asked where I *lived*. No auxiliary "do/does/did" in the reported clause.
   - Question mark is dropped in reported speech.

6. **Commands and Requests:**
   - **Commands** (orders): reporting verb changes to "ordered/commanded/told" + object + **to + infinitive** — e.g., "Sit down!" → He ordered/told them **to sit down**.
   - **Requests** (polite asks, often starting with "please"): reporting verb changes to "requested/begged" + object + **to + infinitive** — e.g., "Please help me." → He requested me **to help** him.
   - **Negative commands/requests:** "not to + infinitive" — e.g., "Don't shout." → He told them **not to shout**.

**Where students go wrong (the biggest unit, and usually the most heavily tested):**
- Forgetting to change question word order to statement word order in reported questions (keeping "did he go" instead of changing to "he went/had gone").
- Using "that" after "asked" for Wh-questions (should be no "that" — just the wh-word directly).
- Forgetting "if/whether" for yes/no questions.
- Not doing the tense backshift when the reporting verb is in the past ("said," "asked," "told").
- Mixing up "said to" (should become "told") vs "said" (stays "said" when no listener is mentioned) — "said to me" → "told me," but plain "said" → stays "said."
- Forgetting to convert imperative sentences into "to + infinitive" structure instead of keeping the exact original verb form.

## Quick reference — the 6 traps most likely to appear in the exam

| # | Trap | Rule to remember |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | must not vs need not | must not = forbidden; need not = optional, not required |
| 2 | a few/little vs few/little | with "a" = some (positive); without "a" = almost none (negative) |
| 3 | present perfect vs simple past | present perfect = unspecified time/present relevance; simple past = specific past time marker |
| 4 | subject–verb agreement with inserted phrases | ignore "along with/as well as/together with" — verb agrees with the real subject only |
| 5 | either...or / neither...nor agreement | verb agrees with the subject nearer to it |
| 6 | reported yes/no questions | use "if/whether" + statement word order, never keep question word order |
