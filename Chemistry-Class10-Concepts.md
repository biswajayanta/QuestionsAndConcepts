## Block 1: Bonding and naming

### 1.1 Why carbon forms covalent bonds
- Carbon has atomic number 6, so its electronic configuration is **2, 4**. It has **4 valence electrons**.
- To get a stable octet it would have to lose 4 electrons (too much energy) or gain 4 electrons (6 protons cannot hold 10 electrons easily).
- So carbon **shares** electrons. A shared pair of electrons is a **covalent bond**.

**Electron dot structure of methane (CH4):** each ':' below is one shared pair.
```
      H
      :
  H : C : H
      :
      H
```
*Common slip:* drawing dots for the electrons that are not shared. In dot structures of simple molecules, show all valence electrons, and make sure each atom ends with an octet (hydrogen with a duet).

### 1.2 Properties of covalent compounds
- **Low melting and boiling points** (the forces between molecules are weak).
- **Poor conductors of electricity** (no ions, no free electrons).
- Generally soluble in organic solvents and not in water.

### 1.3 Allotropes of carbon
| Allotrope | Structure | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| Diamond | Each C bonded to **four** others in a rigid 3-D network | Hardest natural substance, does not conduct |
| Graphite | Each C bonded to **three** others in hexagonal layers; one electron free | Soft and slippery (layers slide), **conducts electricity** |
| Fullerene | Football-shaped cluster, C-60 | Used in nanotechnology |

### 1.4 Why carbon has so many compounds
- **Catenation:** carbon atoms join to each other in long chains, branched chains and rings. Example: propane (C–C–C chain), cyclohexane (ring). The C–C bond is very strong.
- **Tetravalency:** carbon forms **four** bonds, so it can bond with H, O, N, S and Cl. Example: CH4.

### 1.5 Hydrocarbons
- **Saturated** (only single bonds): **alkanes**, general formula **CnH2n+2**.
- **Unsaturated:** alkenes (one double bond), **CnH2n**, and alkynes (one triple bond), **CnH2n−2**.

| No. of C | Alkane | Alkene | Alkyne |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Methane CH4 | n/a | n/a |
| 2 | Ethane C2H6 | Ethene C2H4 | Ethyne C2H2 |
| 3 | Propane C3H8 | Propene C3H6 | Propyne C3H4 |
| 4 | Butane C4H10 | Butene C4H8 | Butyne C4H6 |
| 5 | Pentane C5H12 | | |
| 6 | Hexane C6H14 | | |

**Counting covalent bonds:** in an alkane CnH2n+2 there are (2n + 2) C–H bonds and (n − 1) C–C bonds. Propane: 8 + 2 = 10 bonds. Ethane: 6 + 1 = 7 bonds.

### 1.6 Structural isomers
**Isomers** have the **same molecular formula but different structures**. Butane (C4H10) has two:
```
CH3 – CH2 – CH2 – CH3        n-butane

CH3 – CH – CH3               isobutane (2-methylpropane)
        |
       CH3
```

### 1.7 Functional groups and homologous series
A **functional group** is an atom or group of atoms that decides the chemical properties of a compound.

| Class | Group | Example | Suffix / prefix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alcohol | –OH | CH3CH2OH (ethanol) | -ol |
| Aldehyde | –CHO | CH3CHO (ethanal) | -al |
| Ketone | >C=O | CH3COCH3 (propanone) | -one |
| Carboxylic acid | –COOH | CH3COOH (ethanoic acid) | -oic acid |
| Halo | –Cl, –Br | CH3CH2Cl (chloroethane) | chloro-, bromo- |

**Homologous series:** a family of compounds with the **same functional group**, the **same general formula** and similar chemical properties. Successive members differ by **–CH2–** (a mass of **14 u**). Physical properties (boiling point, for example) change gradually down the series.

### 1.8 IUPAC naming (4 steps)
1. Count the carbon atoms: 1 meth, 2 eth, 3 prop, 4 but, 5 pent, 6 hex.
2. Single bonds give **-ane**, a double bond **-ene**, a triple bond **-yne** (drop the final 'e' if the suffix starts with a vowel: propane + ol = propanol).
3. A functional group changes the ending: -ol, -al, -one, -oic acid.
4. A halogen is a prefix: chloro-, bromo-.

*Examples:* CH3CH2CH2OH = propanol, CH3CH2COOH = propanoic acid, CH3CHO = ethanal, CH3COCH3 = propanone.

*Where it goes wrong:*
- Giving the **name of the compound** when asked for the **functional group** or the **class**. Read the question for exactly which one is asked.
- Calling CH3COCH3 "propanal". An aldehyde ends in -al and has –CHO at the end of the chain. A ketone ends in -one and has C=O in the middle.

## Block 2: Four reaction types

| Type | What happens | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| **Combustion** | Burns in oxygen to give CO2 + H2O + heat and light | CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O |
| **Oxidation** | Gain of oxygen (or loss of hydrogen). Alcohol → carboxylic acid with **alkaline KMnO4 or acidified K2Cr2O7** | CH3CH2OH → CH3COOH |
| **Addition** | Unsaturated + H2, with **Ni (or Pd)** catalyst → saturated. Called **hydrogenation** | CH2=CH2 + H2 → CH3–CH3 |
| **Substitution** | One atom replaced by another, **in sunlight** | CH4 + Cl2 → CH3Cl + HCl |

- **Saturated** hydrocarbons undergo **substitution** (and combustion). **Unsaturated** hydrocarbons undergo **addition**.
- Hydrogenation turns vegetable oils (unsaturated) into saturated fats (vanaspati ghee).

*Where it goes wrong:* swapping addition and substitution; leaving out the catalyst (Ni) or the sunlight on the arrow.

## Block 3: Ethanol and ethanoic acid

### Ethanol (C2H5OH)
| Reaction | Equation | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| With sodium | 2C2H5OH + 2Na → 2C2H5ONa + **H2** | Hydrogen gas burns with a pop sound |
| Dehydration | C2H5OH → (**conc. H2SO4, 443 K**) → CH2=CH2 + H2O | Gives **ethene**. Conc. H2SO4 removes water |
| Oxidation | C2H5OH → (alk. KMnO4 or acidified K2Cr2O7) → CH3COOH | |
| Combustion | C2H5OH + 3O2 → 2CO2 + 3H2O | |

### Ethanoic acid (CH3COOH)
- **Glacial** ethanoic acid freezes at 290 K. **Vinegar** is a 5 to 8% solution in water.
- A weak acid. Turns blue litmus red.

| Reaction | Equation |
|---|---|
| With a base (neutralisation) | CH3COOH + NaOH → CH3COONa + H2O |
| With sodium hydrogencarbonate (releases CO2) | CH3COOH + NaHCO3 → CH3COONa + H2O + CO2 |
| With sodium carbonate | 2CH3COOH + Na2CO3 → 2CH3COONa + H2O + CO2 |
| **Esterification** (with an alcohol, **conc. H2SO4 as catalyst**) | CH3COOH + C2H5OH → CH3COOC2H5 + H2O |

**Distinguishing ethanol from ethanoic acid:** add sodium hydrogencarbonate. Ethanoic acid gives brisk effervescence of CO2 (turns limewater milky). Ethanol gives none.

### Esters and saponification
- **Ester:** formed from an acid + an alcohol. It has a **sweet, fruity smell** and is used in perfumes and as a flavouring.
- Ethanoic acid + ethanol gives **ethyl ethanoate**.
- **Saponification:** ester + NaOH → alcohol + sodium salt of the acid.
CH3COOC2H5 + NaOH → CH3COONa + C2H5OH. This is how soap is made from fats and oils.

*Where it goes wrong:* not **naming the product** (the question says "name"). Not writing the **conditions** (conc. H2SO4, 443 K, Ni, sunlight). Writing H2CO3 as a product of carbonate reactions: it breaks down, so write **H2O + CO2**.

## Block 4: Soaps and detergents

- **Soap:** sodium or potassium salt of a long-chain carboxylic acid.
- A soap molecule has two ends:
  - a **hydrophilic (ionic) head** that likes water,
  - a **hydrophobic (hydrocarbon) tail** that likes oil and grease.
- **Cleansing action:** in water, soap molecules gather with their **tails pointing inward**, around the oily dirt, and their **heads pointing outward** into the water. This ball-shaped cluster is a **micelle**. The dirt is trapped inside. The ionic heads repel each other, so the micelles stay suspended and the dirt is washed away with the water.
- **Hard water** contains calcium and magnesium salts. Soap reacts with them to form an insoluble, sticky **scum**, so less lather forms and more soap is wasted.
- **Detergents** are sodium salts of sulphonic acids, or ammonium salts with chloride or bromide ends. Their calcium and magnesium salts are **soluble**, so they **do not form scum** and work in hard water.

*Where it goes wrong:* mixing up head and tail (the **head** is hydrophilic, the **tail** is hydrophobic); saying "the water is cleaned" (the **dirt** is removed).

# PART 2: CHEMICAL REACTIONS AND EQUATIONS

**Balancing:** the number of atoms of each element must be equal on both sides. Balance one element at a time, and leave hydrogen and oxygen for last. Never change the formulas, only the coefficients.

| Type | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Combination | A + B → AB | 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO |
| Decomposition | AB → A + B | CaCO3 → CaO + CO2 (thermal) |
| Displacement | A + BC → AC + B | Fe + CuSO4 → FeSO4 + Cu |
| Double displacement | AB + CD → AD + CB | Na2SO4 + BaCl2 → BaSO4 + 2NaCl (a precipitate) |

**Decomposition by the energy used:** thermal (heat), electrolytic (electricity), photolytic (light).
- Thermal: 2FeSO4 → Fe2O3 + SO2 + SO3 (green crystals turn reddish-brown).
- Photolytic: 2AgCl → 2Ag + Cl2 (white turns grey in sunlight).

**Exothermic** reactions release heat (combustion, respiration). **Endothermic** reactions absorb heat (photosynthesis, decomposition of CaCO3).

**Oxidation and reduction (redox):** oxidation is gain of oxygen (or loss of hydrogen). Reduction is loss of oxygen (or gain of hydrogen). They happen together.
In CuO + H2 → Cu + H2O: CuO is reduced (it is the **oxidising agent**), and H2 is oxidised (it is the **reducing agent**).

**Corrosion and rancidity:** corrosion is the slow attack on metals (rusting of iron needs air and moisture). Rancidity is the spoiling of fats and oils by oxidation, prevented by antioxidants and nitrogen packaging.

**Also remember:** a magnesium ribbon is cleaned before burning to remove the layer of magnesium oxide on its surface.

*Where it goes wrong:* changing subscripts when balancing; naming the wrong substance as oxidised (find which one gains oxygen); mixing up displacement and double displacement.

# PART 3: ACIDS, BASES AND SALTS

**Indicators:** acids turn blue litmus red. Bases turn red litmus blue. Phenolphthalein is colourless in acid and pink in base.

**Reactions**
- Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen. Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl2 + H2 (the gas burns with a pop sound).
- Acid + metal carbonate/hydrogencarbonate → salt + water + CO2.
- Acid + base → salt + water (**neutralisation**).
- Metal oxide + acid → salt + water, so metal oxides are basic. Non-metal oxide + base → salt + water, so non-metal oxides are acidic.
- Some metals react with bases too: Zn + 2NaOH → Na2ZnO2 + H2.

**Why dry HCl gas does not change dry litmus:** acids show their properties only in water, when they release **H+ (H3O+) ions**.

**pH scale**
- 0 to 14. **Below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral, above 7 is basic.** The lower the pH, the stronger the acid (higher H+ concentration).
- Importance: **digestion** (stomach HCl, antacids), **tooth decay** (below pH 5.5), **plants and animals** (soil pH, acid rain below 5.6), **self-defence** (bee sting: methanoic acid, treated with baking soda).

**Salts:** salts of strong acid + strong base are neutral (pH 7). Salts of strong base + weak acid are basic (pH above 7), e.g. Na2CO3. Salts of strong acid + weak base are acidic (pH below 7), e.g. NH4Cl.

**Chemicals from common salt**
| Chemical | Formula | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Chlor-alkali process:** electrolysis of brine | 2NaCl + 2H2O → 2NaOH + Cl2 + H2 | NaOH: soap, paper. Cl2: PVC, disinfectant. H2: fuel |
| Baking soda | NaHCO3 | Antacid, baking powder, fire extinguishers |
| Washing soda | Na2CO3·10H2O | Washing clothes, softening hard water |
| Bleaching powder | CaOCl2 | Disinfecting water, bleaching |
| Plaster of Paris | CaSO4·½H2O | Casts, toys. Stored in moisture-proof containers (it sets to gypsum, CaSO4·2H2O, with water) |

**Water of crystallisation:** copper sulphate crystals are blue (CuSO4·5H2O). On heating they turn white (anhydrous). Adding water brings back the blue.

*Where it goes wrong:* thinking a lower pH means a weaker acid; forgetting that Zn reacts with NaOH too.

# PART 4: METALS AND NON-METALS

**Physical properties of metals:** lustre, malleable, ductile, good conductors of heat and electricity, high melting points (exceptions: sodium and potassium are soft; mercury is a liquid). Non-metals are the opposite (exceptions: graphite conducts, diamond is hard).

**Chemical properties**
- With oxygen: 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO. Metal oxides are basic. **Amphoteric** oxides (Al2O3, ZnO) react with both acids and bases.
- With water: sodium and potassium react vigorously with cold water. Magnesium reacts with hot water, and aluminium, zinc and iron react with steam. Copper, silver and gold do not react.
- With dilute acids: metals above hydrogen release H2. Copper does not.
- With salt solutions: a **more reactive** metal displaces a less reactive one. Fe + CuSO4 → FeSO4 + Cu.

**Reactivity series (high to low):** K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb, (H), Cu, Hg, Ag, Au.
Sodium and potassium are kept in kerosene because they react vigorously with air and moisture.

**Ionic compounds:** formed by **transfer of electrons**. Na → Na+ + e−, Cl + e− → Cl−. They have **high melting and boiling points** (strong electrostatic forces), are **hard and brittle**, **soluble in water**, and **conduct electricity in molten state and solution but not as solids** (ions are free to move only when molten or dissolved).

**Extraction of metals (by position in the reactivity series)**
| Reactivity | Method | Example |
|---|---|---|
| High (K to Al) | **Electrolytic reduction** of molten chloride | Na+ + e− → Na |
| Medium (Zn, Fe, Pb) | Convert ore to oxide, then **reduce with carbon** | ZnO + C → Zn + CO |
| Low (Cu, Hg, Ag) | Heating | |

- **Roasting:** heating a **sulphide ore** in excess air: 2ZnS + 3O2 → 2ZnO + 2SO2.
- **Calcination:** heating a **carbonate ore** in limited or no air: ZnCO3 → ZnO + CO2.
- Highly reactive metals cannot be reduced by carbon because their oxides are more stable than carbon's.

**Alloys:** a homogeneous mixture of a metal with another metal or non-metal. Examples: brass (Cu + Zn), bronze (Cu + Sn), solder (Pb + Sn).

**Corrosion:** rusting of iron needs air and moisture. Prevented by painting, galvanising (zinc coating) and alloying.

*Where it goes wrong:* confusing roasting and calcination; forgetting that reactivity decides the method of extraction.
