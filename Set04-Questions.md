## Mathematics

**M1. (Pair of Linear Equations — word problem)**
A fraction becomes 4/5 if 1 is added to both numerator and denominator. It becomes 1/2 if 1 is subtracted from both numerator and denominator instead. Find the fraction. (Set up two equations carefully — don't assume which operation goes with which resulting fraction without checking the wording.)

**M2. (Triangles — similarity)**
In triangle ABC, D and E are points on sides AB and AC respectively, such that DE is parallel to BC. If AD = 4 cm, DB = 6 cm, and AE = 5 cm, find EC. Then, if the area of triangle ADE is 20 cm², find the area of triangle ABC — don't just use a length ratio where an area ratio is needed.

**M3. (Coordinate Geometry)**
The points A(2, 3), B(x, y), and C(8, 9) are such that B divides AC in the ratio 2:1. Find the coordinates of B. Then verify your answer by checking that A, B, and C are collinear (i.e., that B actually lies on line AC) — state clearly how you're verifying this, not just asserting it.

**M4. (Circles — tangent properties)**
From an external point P, two tangents PA and PB are drawn to a circle with centre O, touching the circle at A and B respectively. If angle APB = 60°, find angle AOB. Then find angle OAB. Show the reasoning for each step — which property of tangents are you using at each stage?

## Physics

**P1. (Mirrors — sign convention, deliberately paired against the lens work you've already mastered)**
An object 6 cm tall is placed 15 cm from a **concave mirror** of focal length 10 cm.
(a) Find the image distance and magnification, using the **mirror formula** (1/v + 1/u = 1/f) and the **mirror magnification formula** (m = −v/u) — note this is deliberately different from the lens formulas you used in the retouch set. Be careful not to mix them up.
(b) State whether the image is real or virtual, erect or inverted, and enlarged or diminished.
(c) In one sentence, explain the sign-convention rule you used to decide real vs. virtual **for a mirror** — and how it differs from the rule for a lens.

**P2. (Electricity — resistivity and wire design)**
A wire of resistivity 1.6 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m, length 2 m, and cross-sectional area 1 × 10⁻⁶ m² is used as part of a circuit.
(a) Find its resistance.
(b) If you needed to **double** the resistance using a wire of the *same material* and *same cross-sectional area*, what would you need to do to the length? Justify from the resistivity formula — don't just guess "double it," show why.
(c) If instead you kept the length and material the same but wanted to double the resistance by changing the cross-sectional area, what would you need to do to the area? Explain why this changes resistance in the opposite way to length.

**P3. (Light — refraction and real vs. apparent depth)**
A coin lying at the bottom of a beaker filled with water (refractive index 4/3) appears to be at a depth of 12 cm when viewed from directly above.
(a) Find the actual (real) depth of the coin.
(b) Explain, in terms of the direction light bends when passing from a denser to a rarer medium, *why* the apparent depth is less than the real depth (don't just state the formula — explain the ray-bending reasoning behind it).

**P4. (Electricity — household circuits and fuses)**
A household circuit is protected by a 5A fuse and is connected to a 220V supply. The circuit has three appliances that can be switched on at the same time: a 100W bulb, a 750W iron, and a 1500W heater.
(a) If all three appliances are switched on simultaneously, will the fuse blow? Show your calculation (find total current drawn, compare to fuse rating) — don't just add wattages and guess.
(b) Explain, in terms of what a fuse actually does, why using a fuse rated *much higher* than necessary (e.g. a 15A fuse in this same circuit) would be unsafe, even though it would stop the fuse from "blowing" as often.

## Chemistry

**C1. (Chemical Reactions — classification with a twist)**
Consider the reaction: 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s), which occurs with a bright white flame.
(a) Classify this reaction by type (or types — there may be more than one applicable classification, similar to the thermite reaction you analysed earlier).
(b) This reaction is also an oxidation reaction *and* the magnesium can be said to have been "reduced" in one specific sense — wait, is that actually correct? Carefully explain whether magnesium is oxidized or reduced here, and justify using the electron-transfer definition of oxidation/reduction, not just the "gain/loss of oxygen" definition (which one applies more clearly here, and do both definitions agree?).

**C2. (Acids, Bases and Salts — real-life application)**
Tooth enamel is made of calciu​m hydroxyapatite, and starts to get corroded when the pH in the mouth falls below approximately 5.5. Bacteria in the mouth break down sugars left on teeth, producing acids as a by-product.
(a) Explain, step by step, why eating sugary food increases the risk of tooth decay, connecting sugar → bacterial action → pH change → enamel damage. Don't just say "sugar causes cavities" — trace the actual mechanism.
(b) Toothpaste is usually mildly basic. Explain why this specifically helps counter the problem described in (a), using the concept of neutralisation.

**C3. (Metals & Non-metals — ionic compound formation)**
Magnesium (atomic number 12) reacts with chlorine (atomic number 17) to form magnesium chloride.
(a) Write the electronic configurations of Mg and Cl, and explain, in terms of achieving a stable octet, how many electrons Mg loses and how many electrons each Cl atom gains.
(b) Hence explain why the formula of magnesium chloride is MgCl₂ and not MgCl or MgCl₃ — connect this directly to the electron-transfer numbers from part (a).
(c) Ionic compounds like MgCl₂ have high melting points. Explain why, in terms of the forces holding the ions together in the solid.

**C4. (Metals & Non-metals — reactivity with water/steam/acid, prediction)**
Four metals — sodium, iron, copper, and calcium — react differently when exposed to cold water, steam, and dilute acid, based on their position in the reactivity series.
(a) Predict, for **each** of the four metals, whether it reacts with cold water, only with steam, only with dilute acid, or not at all with any of these (some metals may fit more than one category — think about what "reacts with cold water" implies about reacting with steam and acid too).
(b) Explain the general reactivity-series principle that lets you make these predictions without memorising each reaction separately.

## Biology

**B1. (Life Processes — double circulation)**
Human beings have a "double circulation" system — blood passes through the heart twice in one complete cycle around the body (once via the pulmonary circuit, once via the systemic circuit).
(a) Briefly describe the path of blood in each of the two circuits (name the heart chambers and major vessels involved, in order).
(b) Explain *why* this double circulation (as opposed to a single circuit, as in fish) is necessary for efficient functioning in warm-blooded animals like humans — connect it to the need to keep oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate.

**B2. (Life Processes — transportation in plants)**
On a hot, dry day, a plant's rate of water loss through its leaves (transpiration) increases significantly, and this is what actually pulls water upward from the roots through the xylem.
(a) Explain the mechanism by which increased transpiration at the leaf actually causes water to rise through the xylem all the way from the roots — what force is being generated, and how does it act through a continuous column of water?
(b) If a plant's stomata were experimentally forced to stay completely closed all day, predict what would happen to the rate of water movement up the stem, and explain why.

**B3. (Control & Coordination — endocrine system)**
The thyroid gland secretes thyroxine, which regulates the body's basal metabolic rate. If the thyroid gland is not producing enough iodine-containing thyroxine (often due to iodine deficiency in the diet), a person's metabolism slows down, and the thyroid gland itself may swell up (a condition called goitre).
(a) Explain why iodine deficiency specifically leads to reduced thyroxine production (connect iodine's chemical role to thyroxine's structure/function).
(b) Suggest a plausible explanation for *why* the thyroid gland swells up (produces goitre) specifically when it isn't making enough thyroxine, rather than just producing less hormone quietly. (Hint: think about feedback — what signal does the body send to the thyroid gland when hormone levels are low, and what might happen if the gland tries to respond to that signal but lacks the raw material to succeed?)

**B4. (Reproduction — asexual vs. sexual)**
Some organisms (like Amoeba, Hydra, and many plants via cuttings) can reproduce asexually, producing offspring genetically identical to the parent. Others must reproduce sexually, involving two parents and genetic mixing.
(a) Name and briefly describe two different modes of asexual reproduction seen in different organisms (e.g. one for a unicellular organism, one for a multicellular one).
(b) Explain one genuine advantage and one genuine disadvantage of asexual reproduction compared to sexual reproduction, in terms of speed/reliability versus genetic diversity — connect this to the same "genetic diversity matters for survival" logic you used in an earlier pollination question, rather than treating this as an unrelated new idea.

## Social Science

**S1. (History — The Making of a Global World)**
Historians point out that trade, migration, and the spread of disease were deeply connected during the pre-modern phase of globalisation (roughly before the 19th century) — for example, the transfer of diseases like smallpox to the Americas following European contact had a bigger impact on the conquest of the Americas than European military technology alone.
Explain, with reasoning, how disease specifically contributed to European conquest of the Americas — why would a population lacking prior exposure to a disease be catastrophically more vulnerable than the population that carried it? Don't just state that "many people died" — explain the biological/epidemiological reasoning behind the vulnerability gap.

**S2. (Geography — Forest and Wildlife Resources)**
Conservationists classify Indian wildlife/plant species into categories such as "endangered," "vulnerable," and "rare," rather than using a single "at risk" label for all of them.
Explain why having multiple distinct categories (rather than one single at-risk label) is actually useful for conservation planning — what different kind of action or urgency might each category imply? Give at least two genuinely distinct reasons why finer categorisation helps.

**S3. (Civics — Federalism)**
India is often described as a federal system with "unitary features" — meaning it isn't a pure federation like some other countries.
Give **two specific examples** of features in the Indian constitutional/political system that lean toward centralisation (i.e., give the central government more power relative to the states) — and briefly explain why each one counts as a departure from pure federalism, not just describe what it is.

**S4. (Economics — Money and Credit)**
Formal sources of credit (banks, cooperatives) are generally considered better for borrowers than informal sources (moneylenders, traders), even though informal credit is often faster and easier to access.
Explain, using at least two distinct reasons, why formal credit is generally considered more beneficial for a borrower's long-term financial wellbeing than informal credit — go beyond just "lower interest rates" and explain the underlying mechanisms (e.g. related to regulation, debt traps, or terms of repayment).
