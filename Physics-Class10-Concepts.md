## Block 1: Magnet and field lines

- A **magnetic field** is the region around a magnet where its force can be felt. A **compass needle** is a tiny bar magnet, and it lines up with the field.
- **Field lines** show the direction of the field. The direction of the field at a point is the direction a north pole would move there.

**Properties of field lines (learn these):**
1. Outside the magnet they **emerge from the north pole and merge at the south pole**. Inside the magnet they run from south to north, so they are **closed curves**.
2. The **tangent** to a field line at a point gives the direction of the field there.
3. Where the lines are **closer together**, the field is **stronger** (near the poles).
4. **Two field lines never cross.** If they did, a compass needle at the crossing point would have to point in two directions at once.

```
        ___________
      /  /‾‾‾‾‾‾\  \
     |  |  N  S  |  |        Outside: N → S
      \  \______/  /         Inside:  S → N
        ‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾
```
*Where it goes wrong:* drawing field lines that stop at the poles (they are closed loops); drawing arrows inside the magnet the wrong way round; drawing lines that cross.

## Block 2: Straight current-carrying wire

- **Oersted's experiment (Hans Christian Oersted):** a compass needle near a wire carrying current is deflected. So **a current produces a magnetic field**.
- Around a straight wire, the field lines are **concentric circles** centred on the wire.

**Right-hand thumb rule:** imagine holding the wire in your right hand with your **thumb pointing in the direction of the current**. Your **fingers wrap round the wire in the direction of the field lines**.

*Example:* current flowing vertically upward. Seen from above, the field lines run **anticlockwise**.

| Change | Effect on the field at a point |
|---|---|
| Current **increases** | Field gets **stronger** |
| Distance from the wire **increases** | Field gets **weaker** |
| Current **reversed** | Field **direction reverses** (the strength stays the same) |

*Where it goes wrong:* using the thumb rule to find **force**. The thumb rule gives the **field**. Force needs Fleming's rule (Block 4).

## Block 3: Circular loop and solenoid

### Circular loop
- Each small piece of the loop gives circular field lines. Near the wire the lines are circles, and they get larger further out. **At the centre of the loop the field lines are straight lines, perpendicular to the plane of the loop.**
- The field at the centre is **stronger** if: the **current** is larger, the **radius** of the loop is **smaller**, or there are **more turns** (a coil of n turns gives n times the field of one turn).

### Solenoid
- A **solenoid** is a long coil of many circular turns of insulated copper wire, wound closely in the shape of a cylinder.
- Its field looks like that of a **bar magnet**: one end acts as a **north pole** and the other as a **south pole**.
- **Inside the solenoid the field lines are parallel straight lines**, so the field is **uniform** (the same strength at every point).

```
   ┌───────────────────────────┐
   │  →   →   →   →   →   →    │   Inside: parallel straight lines (uniform field)
   └───────────────────────────┘   Outside: curves back, like a bar magnet
     S                       N
```

- **Strength of a solenoid's field** increases with: more **turns**, larger **current**, and a **soft iron core**.
- A **soft iron core** inside the solenoid makes an **electromagnet**. It is a **temporary** magnet: it is magnetised only while current flows. A permanent magnet is made of steel.
- **Reversing the current** reverses the field, so the north and south ends swap.

*Where it goes wrong:* saying the field inside a solenoid is weak (it is strong and uniform); using the wrong core material (soft iron for an electromagnet, steel for a permanent magnet).

## Block 4: Force on a current-carrying conductor

- A current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field **experiences a force**. (Ampère suggested that a magnet should exert a force on a current-carrying wire, as the wire exerts a force on a magnet.)
- The force is **largest when the current is at right angles to the field**.
- **The direction of the force reverses** if the **current** is reversed, or if the **field** is reversed. (Reverse both and the force is unchanged.)

**Fleming's left-hand rule:** stretch the **thumb, forefinger and central finger of the left hand so that they are mutually perpendicular**.
- **Forefinger** → direction of the **magnetic field**
- **Central finger** → direction of the **current**
- **Thumb** → direction of the **force** (the motion of the conductor)

*Memory aid:* **F**ield – **F**orefinger, **C**urrent – **C**entral finger, **T**humb – **T**hrust (force).

*Worked example:* field points down, current flows east. Forefinger points down, central finger points east, so the thumb points **north**. Reverse the current and the force points **south**.

*Where it goes wrong:* using the **right** hand; mixing up which finger is the field and which is the current.

## Block 5: Direct and alternating current

| | DC (direct current) | AC (alternating current) |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Flows in **one direction** only | **Reverses direction** periodically |
| Source | Cells and batteries | Mains supply |
| In India | n/a | **50 Hz**: the direction reverses **every 1/100 second** (twice in each cycle, so 100 times per second) |

- **Advantage of AC over DC:** AC can be **transmitted over long distances without much loss of energy**.

*Where it goes wrong:* saying AC reverses 50 times per second. Frequency 50 Hz means 50 complete cycles per second, and the direction reverses **twice per cycle**, so **100 times per second**.

## Block 6: Domestic electric circuits

- Mains supply in India is **220 V, 50 Hz**.
- **Three wires:**

| Wire | Insulation colour | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Live** | **Red** | Brings current to the appliance (at high potential) |
| **Neutral** | **Black** | Returns the current (at zero potential) |
| **Earth** | **Green** | Safety wire, joined to a metal plate deep in the earth |

- The p.d. between the live and neutral wires is **220 V**.
- Mains passes through a **fuse**, then to the distribution box. Appliances are connected in **parallel**, so each gets the same potential difference (220 V) and can be switched on and off independently.
- **Two separate circuits:** about **15 A** for high-power appliances (geyser, AC), about **5 A** for low-power ones (bulbs, fans).

**Earth wire:** it is connected to the **metal body** of an appliance. If a fault makes the body live, the leaking current flows to the earth through this low-resistance path, so the body stays at **earth potential** and the user does **not get a shock**.

**Short circuit:** the live and neutral wires touch directly (worn insulation or a fault in the appliance). The resistance becomes very small, so a **very large current** flows.

**Overloading:** too many appliances connected to one socket (or a sudden rise in supply voltage) draws a current above the safe limit.

**Fuse:** a piece of wire of an alloy with an **appropriately low melting point**, joined **in series** in the circuit. Too much current heats it (**heating effect of current**), so it **melts and breaks the circuit**, protecting the appliances.

*Where it goes wrong:* putting the fuse in parallel; saying the fuse "stops" the current without saying it melts; mixing up short circuit and overloading.

# PART 2: ELECTRICITY

**Key relations**
| Quantity | Formula | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Current | I = Q / t | ampere (A) |
| Potential difference | V = W / Q | volt (V). **1 V = 1 J per coulomb** |
| **Ohm's law** | V = I R (at constant temperature) | |
| Resistance | R = ρ l / A | ohm (Ω) |
| Resistivity | ρ | Ω m |
| Power | P = V I = I²R = V² / R | watt (W) |
| Heat produced (**Joule's law**) | H = I² R t | joule (J) |
| Electrical energy | E = P × t | **1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J** |

**Ohm's law (statement):** at constant temperature, the **current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across its ends**.

**Resistance depends on:** length (R ∝ l), area of cross-section (R ∝ 1/A), the material (ρ) and temperature.

| | Series | Parallel |
|---|---|---|
| Current | **Same** through all | **Divides** among branches |
| p.d. | **Divides** (adds up) | **Same** across all |
| Equivalent resistance | R = R1 + R2 + … | 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + … |
| Size | Larger than the biggest | **Smaller than the smallest** |

- **Ammeter** in **series**. **Voltmeter** in **parallel**.
- **Alloys** (nichrome) are used in heating elements: **high resistivity**, **high melting point**, and they **do not oxidise (burn) readily** when hot. **Tungsten** is used in bulb filaments (very high melting point). **Copper and aluminium** are used in wires (low resistivity).
- **Why household appliances are in parallel:** same voltage for each, independent switching, and if one fails the others keep working.

*Where it goes wrong:* adding resistances instead of their reciprocals in parallel; forgetting to convert hours and watts to kWh; writing the resistivity unit as Ω instead of Ω m.

# PART 3: LIGHT: REFLECTION AND REFRACTION

## Spherical mirrors
- **Pole (P)**, **centre of curvature (C)**, **principal focus (F)**, **focal length f**. **R = 2f**.
- **Cartesian sign convention:** all distances measured **from the pole**. Distances in the direction of the **incident light are positive**; against it, negative. Heights **upward** are positive.
  - **Concave mirror: f is negative.** **Convex mirror: f is positive.**
- **Mirror formula:** **1/v + 1/u = 1/f**   **Magnification:** **m = h′/h = −v/u**
- **Rays used for diagrams (concave mirror):** (1) parallel to the axis → reflects through F; (2) through F → reflects parallel to the axis; (3) through C → reflects back along itself.

**Concave mirror: images**
| Object position | Image position | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| At infinity | At F | Real, inverted, highly diminished (a point) |
| Beyond C | Between F and C | Real, inverted, diminished |
| At C | At C | Real, inverted, same size |
| Between C and F | Beyond C | Real, inverted, enlarged |
| At F | At infinity | Real, inverted, highly enlarged |
| Between P and F | Behind the mirror | **Virtual, erect, enlarged** |

**Convex mirror:** the image is **always virtual, erect and diminished**, between P and F behind the mirror. This gives a **wider field of view**, so it is used as a **rear-view mirror**.

## Refraction
- **Laws:**
  1. The incident ray, the refracted ray and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the **same plane**.
  2. **Snell's law:** sin i / sin r = a **constant** (for a given colour and pair of media).
- **Refractive index:** **n = c / v** (speed of light in vacuum ÷ speed in the medium).
- Going from a **rarer to a denser** medium, light **bends towards the normal**. Going from denser to rarer, it bends away.
- **Rectangular glass slab:** the emergent ray is **parallel to the incident ray**, but **shifted sideways** (**lateral displacement**).

## Lenses
- **Convex lens** converges parallel rays. **Concave lens** diverges them.
- **Lens formula:** **1/v − 1/u = 1/f**   **Magnification:** **m = h′/h = v/u**
- **Convex lens: f is positive.** **Concave lens: f is negative.** A real image has positive v (for a convex lens); a virtual image has negative v.
- **Power:** **P = 1/f** (f in **metres**). Unit: **dioptre (D)**. Convex lens: positive power. Concave lens: negative power. For lenses in contact, **P = P1 + P2**.
- **Rays used for diagrams (convex lens):** (1) parallel to the axis → through F; (2) through the optical centre → straight, undeviated; (3) through F → parallel to the axis.

**Convex lens: images**
| Object position | Image position | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| At infinity | At F2 | Real, inverted, highly diminished |
| Beyond 2F1 | Between F2 and 2F2 | Real, inverted, diminished |
| At 2F1 | At 2F2 | Real, inverted, same size |
| Between F1 and 2F1 | Beyond 2F2 | Real, inverted, enlarged |
| At F1 | At infinity | Real, inverted, highly enlarged |
| Between F1 and O | Same side as the object | **Virtual, erect, enlarged** |

A **concave lens** always gives a **virtual, erect, diminished** image between the optical centre and F1.

*Where it goes wrong:* wrong signs (f of a concave mirror is negative; u is always negative for a real object); using centimetres in the power formula (convert to metres); forgetting arrows on rays; mixing up lens formula (1/v − 1/u) and mirror formula (1/v + 1/u).

# PART 4: THE HUMAN EYE AND THE COLOURFUL WORLD

## The eye
- **Cornea** (most refraction happens here), **iris** (controls the pupil), **pupil** (admits light), **eye lens** (fine-focuses the image), **ciliary muscles** (change the lens's focal length), **retina** (screen where the real, inverted image forms), **optic nerve** (carries signals to the brain).
- **Accommodation:** the eye lens changes its focal length so that both near and far objects focus on the retina.
- **Near point** of a normal eye: **25 cm**. **Far point**: **infinity**.

## Defects of vision
| Defect | Problem | Image forms | Causes | Correction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Myopia** (short-sightedness) | Can't see **distant** objects clearly | **In front of** the retina | Eye lens too curved (focal length too short), or eyeball too long | **Concave** lens |
| **Hypermetropia** (long-sightedness) | Can't see **nearby** objects clearly | **Behind** the retina | Focal length too long, or eyeball too short | **Convex** lens |
| **Presbyopia** | Difficulty seeing both near and far in old age | | Ciliary muscles weaken, lens loses flexibility | **Bifocal** lens |

**Cataract:** the lens becomes cloudy, giving blurred vision, and is treated by surgery.

## Prism and dispersion
- A **prism** bends light twice. White light splits into its component colours, called **dispersion**. The order is **VIBGYOR**, from violet to red. **Red deviates least; violet deviates most.**
- A **rainbow** is caused by dispersion of sunlight by tiny water droplets in the air.

## Scattering of light
- **Tyndall effect:** the scattering of light by colloidal particles, which makes the path of a light beam visible (for example, a beam of sunlight entering a dusty room through a small hole).
- **Why the sky is blue:** the molecules of air and other fine particles scatter the **shorter wavelengths (blue)** much more strongly than the longer (red) wavelengths.

*Where it goes wrong:* writing the lenses the wrong way round (myopia needs a **concave** lens); saying "image forms **on** the retina" for a defect (it forms in front of or behind it); saying "dispersion" when the question is about scattering.
