## P4R-1. Convex lens — object beyond 2f... actually between f and 2f (isolating the sign-convention fix)

**Core concept — the exact fix needed:** For a **lens**, magnification is **m = v/u** (no minus sign). This is different from the **mirror** formula, which is m = −v/u. Mixing these up is exactly what happened in Set 03 (P4). The sign of v alone tells you real vs. virtual for a lens: **v positive → real image** (opposite side from object); **v negative → virtual image** (same side as object). The sign of m then tells you erect/inverted: **m negative → inverted; m positive → erect.**

**Correct approach:**
1. u = −40, f = +20. 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/20 + (−1/40) = (2−1)/40 = 1/40 → **v = +40 cm**.
2. m = v/u = 40/(−40) = **−1**.
3. v is positive → **real image**. m is negative → **inverted**. |m| = 1 → **same size** as object (not enlarged, not diminished) — this is the special case where object is exactly at 2f, image forms at 2f on the other side, same size, real, inverted.
4. Rule: for a lens, read real/virtual directly from the **sign of v** (positive=real, negative=virtual) — don't apply the mirror's −v/u formula to a lens question.

**Final answer:** v=+40cm, m=−1, image is real, inverted, same size as object (5cm).

**Check his working against this:** does he write m=v/u (not m=−v/u)? Does he correctly state "v positive → real" as his rule in part (d)?

## P4R-2. Convex lens — object inside focal length (magnifying glass case)

**Correct approach:**
1. u = −10, f = +20. 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/20 + (−1/10) = (1−2)/20 = −1/20 → **v = −20 cm**.
2. m = v/u = (−20)/(−10) = **+2**.
3. v is negative → **virtual image**. m is positive → **erect**. |m| = 2 → **enlarged**.
4. What changed from P4R-1: here the object is *inside* the focal length (10cm < f=20cm), so the lens can't converge the rays to a real point — they only appear to diverge from a virtual point on the same side as the object. This is the magnifying-glass case. The sign of v flipped (negative here vs. positive in P4R-1) because the object crossed from outside f to inside f.

**Final answer:** v=−20cm, m=+2, image is virtual, erect, enlarged.

## B3R-1. Tropisms — precise auxin mechanism

**Correct approach:**
1. (a) The stem will bend **upward, against gravity** (negative geotropism/gravitropism) — light is ruled out as the cause here since it's coming from directly overhead with no sideways asymmetry, so any sideways bending must be a gravity response, not a light response.
2. (b) When the stem lies horizontal, auxin (produced at the growing tip) redistributes under gravity's influence so that a **higher concentration accumulates on the lower side** of the stem than on the upper side. Auxin promotes cell elongation — so the cells on the **lower side elongate faster/more** than cells on the upper side. This unequal elongation (more growth on the bottom, less on top) causes the stem to curve **upward** over time.

**Final answer:** Negative geotropism; auxin concentrates more on the lower side → faster elongation there → upward curving. (The key precision check: does he explicitly say "more on the lower side," not just "auxin moves upward"?)

## B4R-1. Excretion — reabsorption capacity limit

**Correct approach:**
1. In both people, glucose is freely filtered at the glomerulus into the nephron tubule, along with water, salts, and urea.
2. In the **proximal convoluted tubule**, glucose is normally **reabsorbed** back into the blood via specific transport proteins. This reabsorption system has a **maximum capacity** — it can only reabsorb glucose up to a certain rate/amount.
3. Person A (healthy): blood glucose is normal, so the amount of glucose filtered is well within what the tubule's reabsorption system can handle — **all of it gets reabsorbed**, and none appears in the urine.
4. Person B (diabetic): blood glucose is abnormally high, so a much larger amount of glucose is filtered at the glomerulus — more than the tubule's reabsorption capacity can handle. The reabsorption system reabsorbs as much as it can, but the **excess above its capacity is left behind** in the tubule fluid and passes on into the final urine.
5. The key point: it's not that "diabetics stop reabsorbing glucose" — the reabsorption process is the same in both people. What differs is the **amount filtered**, and whether that amount stays within or exceeds the fixed reabsorption capacity.

**Final answer:** Both people reabsorb glucose the same way, but B's much higher filtered glucose exceeds the tubule's fixed reabsorption capacity, so the excess spills into urine — A's filtered glucose stays within that capacity.

## SST1R-1. Print Culture — a second, distinct mechanism

**Core concept:** Beyond cost/reach, printing enabled **vernacular (local-language) publishing** and helped **standardize regional languages**, which is a mechanism of a genuinely different kind — it's about identity and shared community, not just volume of copies.

**Correct approach (any one well-explained mechanism, e.g.):**
1. **Vernacular/language standardization:** Before print, religious and political texts were often confined to Latin (Europe) or Sanskrit/Persian (India) understood only by elites. Printing increasingly published material in everyday vernacular languages, which (a) let ordinary people read/understand ideas directly for the first time, and (b) helped standardize a shared regional language across a dispersed population — and a shared standardized language fostered a sense of common identity, which is a foundational ingredient of nationalism (people could now imagine themselves as part of one linguistic/cultural community).
2. (Other acceptable answers: printing allowed religious reform movements to circulate their own interpretations directly to the public, bypassing traditional religious authorities who had previously controlled access to sacred texts — encouraging independent, critical thinking rather than reliance on institutional intermediaries; or, print allowed rapid, near-simultaneous circulation of the same material across a wide and dispersed population, letting geographically separated people feel part of one shared national conversation/event at the same time.)

**Final answer:** Any genuinely distinct mechanism (language standardization, bypassing religious/institutional gatekeeping, simultaneous shared circulation) — the test is that it must be a different underlying idea from "cheaper and reaches more people," not a restatement of it.
